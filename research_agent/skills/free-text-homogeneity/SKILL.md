# Free-Text Response Homogeneity

## What it is

When AI agents with different personas answer the same free-text question, their responses often sound more similar to each other than the persona variation would suggest. Common symptoms:

- Many responses start with the same opener: "Honestly,", "I think,", "As someone who...", "I believe,", "Well, it depends..."
- Responses follow the same structural template: acknowledge the question → state a position → hedge → conclude with a reflection
- Vocabulary and sentence length are similar across agents even when their trait configurations differ sharply
- A reader looking at the responses side-by-side cannot easily tell which persona wrote which

This is not a bug. It is a predictable property of how large language models work, and it affects every AI simulation study that uses free-text questions.

---

## Why it happens

All agents in an EDSL study are generated by the same underlying language model. That model was trained — through RLHF and related techniques — to produce responses that sound helpful, balanced, and thoughtful. That training creates a distinctive "assistant voice" with characteristic opening phrases, hedging patterns, and structural habits.

Persona conditioning via agent traits and instructions can shift *what* the model says — the position taken, the specific experiences cited, the level of enthusiasm or skepticism — but it has limited power to override *how* the model writes at the level of syntactic patterns and stylistic defaults. The model's learned voice is strong and persists even when the persona instruction specifies a very different communication style.

The problem is compounded by a second mechanism: LLMs generate tokens by predicting the most probable next token given the context. Certain opening phrases ("Honestly,", "I think") have high probability after a survey question prompt regardless of who is supposedly answering. The model has seen these phrases so often in survey-like contexts that they are the path of least resistance.

---

## What it means for results — and what it does not mean

**What it does NOT mean:**

- It does not mean the personas hold the same views. Content differentiation — the positions taken, the details cited, the priorities stated — can still vary meaningfully across segments even when the stylistic surface is similar.
- It does not invalidate scale or multiple-choice question findings. Those question types do not rely on stylistic differentiation at all.
- It does not mean the study is worthless. Substantive variation can coexist with stylistic uniformity.

**What it DOES mean:**

- For free-text questions used qualitatively (verbatim quotes in reports): the quotes illustrate the model's house style, not authentic variation in how differently-profiled people would express themselves. Readers who notice that five "very different" personas all begin with "Honestly," will reasonably wonder what the simulation is actually capturing.
- For the method's credibility: if homogeneity is visible and not explained, a skeptical reader may extend their skepticism to the quantitative findings too — unfairly, but understandably.
- For qualitative richness: the open-ended responses will tend to sound more like competent survey responses than like genuine individual voices. That matters if part of the study's value is giving the reader a feel for how different people actually talk about a subject.

The right response is honest disclosure, targeted mitigation, and calibrated interpretation — not silence and not overstating the problem.

## Design mitigations — things to do before running

These are not guaranteed to eliminate homogeneity. They reduce it at the margin. Apply them when the free-text quality matters to the study's goals.

### 1. Give agents a voice, not just a profile

The most common weak point is the agent instruction string. A typical instruction specifies what the agent *is* (demographics, attitudes, behaviors) but not how they *speak*. Add stylistic and communication-style guidance for each distinct segment.

**Before (generic — produces homogeneous style):**
```
You are a 52-year-old Gen X marketing director with 25 years of experience.
Answer questions from the perspective of someone with your background.
```

**After (adds voice — reduces homogeneity):**
```
You are a 52-year-old Gen X marketing director with 25 years of experience.
You are direct and impatient with vague answers. You speak in short,
declarative sentences. You draw on specific past experiences rather than
generalizing. You do not hedge unless genuinely uncertain, and you rarely
open with qualifications. When you disagree, you say so plainly.
```

Voice dimensions worth varying across segments:
- Sentence length (short and punchy vs. long and qualified)
- Hedging pattern (confident and direct vs. nuanced and cautious)
- Register (casual and colloquial vs. formal and precise)
- Specificity (anecdote-driven vs. abstract/conceptual)
- Emotional expressiveness (dry and analytical vs. personally invested)

### 2. Use `answering_instructions` on free-text questions

EDSL's `answering_instructions` parameter on `QuestionFreeText` gives the model per-question style guidance. This is applied at inference time and can override the model's default opener patterns.

```python
from edsl import QuestionFreeText

q = QuestionFreeText(
    question_name="main_concern",
    question_text="What is your main concern about this policy?",
    answering_instructions=(
        "Answer in 2-3 sentences. Be specific — name a concrete concern, "
        "not a general category. Do not begin your response with 'Honestly,' "
        "'I think,' 'As a,' or similar filler phrases. Go straight to the point."
    )
)
```

Common instructions that reduce opener homogeneity:
- "Do not begin with 'Honestly,' 'I think,' 'As a,' or 'I believe.' Start with the substance."
- "Answer in 1-2 direct sentences."
- "Be specific. Name a concrete example or experience. Avoid general statements."
- "Write the way you would actually speak, not the way a survey respondent would write."

### 3. Write questions that resist boilerplate

Generic question prompts invite generic openers. Questions that demand something specific are harder to open with "Honestly,":

| Generic (produces boilerplate) | Specific (resists boilerplate) |
|---|---|
| "What do you think about this product?" | "What was the last time this product frustrated you? Describe what happened." |
| "How do you feel about the price?" | "At what price would you stop considering this? What makes you say that?" |
| "What would you change about this service?" | "Name one specific thing. What would be different after the change?" |
| "Why do you feel that way?" | "Walk me through the experience that led to that view." |

Questions that demand a story, a specific memory, a single concrete thing, or a number are hard to answer with the assistant's default opener patterns.

### 4. Use multiple models for the same agent panel

If the study includes free-text questions and the client will read the verbatim responses, <!-- lint-allow-model: illustrative example, not a model recommendation -->
consider running the same agent panel through two different models (e.g., GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet). The two models have different stylistic defaults and will not share the same opening phrase patterns. Results can be compared; divergence is itself interesting.

This doubles the cost of the free-text portion but produces demonstrably more linguistic diversity. Disclose the multi-model design in Methods.

### 5. Do not reduce temperature on free-text questions

Lower temperature increases determinism and makes stylistic homogeneity worse — the model more reliably converges on its highest-probability opener. Keep temperature at 1.0 (the default) for free-text questions.

## Detection — things to check after running

Run this check as part of the post-run review for any study with free-text questions.

```python
import pandas as pd
from collections import Counter

# Load results to pandas
df = results.select("agent.agent_name", "answer.q_your_freetext").to_pandas()
df = df.dropna(subset=["answer.q_your_freetext"])
responses = df["answer.q_your_freetext"].str.strip()

# 1. Check for common openers
common_openers = [
    "honestly", "i think", "as a", "i believe", "well,",
    "as someone", "it depends", "i feel", "i would say", "in my opinion"
]
print("=== Opener analysis ===")
for opener in common_openers:
    matches = responses.str.lower().str.startswith(opener)
    if matches.any():
        print(f"  '{opener}': {matches.sum()} / {len(responses)} responses ({matches.mean():.0%})")

# 2. Check for shared 5-word prefix (strong signal of template-like responses)
prefix_5 = responses.str.lower().str.split().apply(lambda x: " ".join(x[:5]) if len(x) >= 5 else " ".join(x))
prefix_counts = prefix_5.value_counts()
shared = prefix_counts[prefix_counts > 1]
if not shared.empty:
    print(f"\n=== Shared 5-word prefixes ({len(shared)} unique, {shared.sum()} total matches) ===")
    print(shared.head(10))
else:
    print("\n=== No shared 5-word prefixes found ===")

# 3. Summary sentence for reporting
total = len(responses)
opener_hits = sum(
    responses.str.lower().str.startswith(op).sum()
    for op in common_openers
)
# (responses can match more than one opener, so this overcounts — use as rough indicator)
print(f"\nRough opener overlap: ~{opener_hits} opener instances across {total} responses")
```

### Interpreting the output

| Signal | What it means | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| >30% of responses share the same opener | Visible homogeneity — disclose in report | High |
| >1 shared 5-word prefix | Strong template pattern — mention in limitations | Medium-High |
| Openers distributed but structural pattern identical | Surface variation, same template — note in review | Medium |
| No pattern found | Homogeneity is not a significant issue for this study | Low |

## How to disclose this honestly in reports

Homogeneity should be disclosed in the Limitations section. The goal is to be accurate without overstating the problem. Do not suppress the finding, but also do not imply it undermines the entire study.

**Template for Limitations section:**

> The free-text responses in this study were generated by [model name], a single underlying language model. Like all AI simulation studies using free-text questions, responses show some surface-level stylistic similarity across agents — for example, [specific observation: e.g., "approximately a third of responses begin with 'Honestly,'"; or "responses tend to follow an acknowledge-then-qualify structure"]. This is a known property of current LLMs: persona conditioning reliably shifts the content of responses (the positions taken, the specific reasons cited, the priorities expressed) but does not reliably produce the distinct communication styles that real people with different backgrounds would use.
>
> This limitation primarily affects how the verbatim quotes should be read: they illustrate substantive variation across segments, not authentic differences in how real people in these demographics would actually express themselves. The quantitative findings from scale and multiple-choice questions are not affected by this issue.

Replace the bracketed sections with the specific findings from your detection check. If the homogeneity is mild, say so. If it is significant, say so.

**What NOT to do:**

- Do not cherry-pick the three most stylistically distinct quotes and present them as representative of the full range. The verbatim examples included in the report should be representative, not optimized for variety.
- Do not omit the Limitations disclosure because the homogeneity "wasn't that bad." If the study used free-text questions and you ran the detection check, include a sentence.
- Do not claim that agent instructions produced "genuinely distinct voices" unless you have specific evidence that they did (e.g., a multi-model run showing stylistic divergence, or a blind rater study).

---

## The conversation with the user

When a study includes free-text questions, raise homogeneity proactively — in the plan, not after the run. A natural way to do it in Plan Section 7:

> "The survey includes [N] free-text questions. AI agents sometimes produce responses with similar opening phrases and sentence structures across personas — this is a known limitation called response homogeneity. I've added `answering_instructions` to each free-text question to reduce this. The report's Limitations section will disclose what we observe. If you want to invest more in linguistic variation, we could write detailed voice guides for each agent segment, or run a second model for comparison — but neither is necessary for the substantive findings."

This tells the user what to expect before they see the results, rather than having them notice the pattern themselves and wonder whether the study is flawed.

## Quick reference

| Stage | Action |
|---|---|
| Plan | Flag homogeneity risk if study has free-text questions; mention mitigation steps |
| Design | Add voice/style guidance to agent instructions; add `answering_instructions` to free-text questions; write specific rather than generic question prompts |
| Post-run | Run the detection script; record opener rates and shared prefix findings |
| Report | Include Limitations disclosure using the template above; do not cherry-pick quotes |
| User conversation | Frame as a known, manageable limitation — not a fatal flaw — and distinguish its effect on free-text vs. scale questions |
