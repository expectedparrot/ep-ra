# Sociological Theory Catalog

Reference catalog for selecting theories in sociological analysis studies. Organized by level of analysis.

## Contents

1. Micro-level theories (individual behavior and interaction)
2. Meso-level theories (groups, organizations, networks)
3. Macro-level theories (social systems, structures, institutions)
4. Cross-level theories
5. Selection guidance

## 1. Micro-Level Theories

### Rational Choice Theory

- **Core idea:** Individuals act to maximize expected utility given their preferences, information, and constraints.
- **Key mechanisms:** Cost-benefit calculation, incentive response, strategic interaction (game theory).
- **Predicts:** Actors respond to changing incentives. Cooperation requires aligned incentives or enforceable contracts. Free-riding in collective action. Exit when costs exceed benefits.
- **Strengths:** Clear, falsifiable predictions. Good for economic behavior, policy response, strategic interaction.
- **Weaknesses:** Assumes stable preferences and good information. Underestimates norms, identity, and emotion. Predictions often too cynical about cooperation.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves clear incentives, strategic interaction, or collective action problems.

### Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner)

- **Core idea:** People categorize themselves into groups, derive self-esteem from group membership, and favor in-groups over out-groups.
- **Key mechanisms:** Social categorization, in-group favoritism, out-group derogation, identity threat response.
- **Predicts:** Intergroup competition even without material conflict. Resistance to changes that threaten group identity. Polarization along group lines. Stronger group cohesion under external threat.
- **Strengths:** Explains irrational loyalty, intergroup conflict, resistance to integration.
- **Weaknesses:** Difficulty predicting which identity dimension becomes salient. Less useful for intragroup dynamics.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves group boundaries, identity, intergroup relations, or organizational mergers/restructuring.

### Symbolic Interactionism (Goffman, Blumer)

- **Core idea:** People act based on the meanings they assign to situations, and meanings are constructed through social interaction.
- **Key mechanisms:** Definition of the situation, impression management, framing, labeling, negotiated order.
- **Predicts:** The same objective situation produces different behaviors depending on how actors define it. Labels and frames shape outcomes (self-fulfilling prophecy). Actors manage impressions strategically.
- **Strengths:** Explains how meaning-making shapes behavior. Good for understanding framing effects, stigma, identity construction.
- **Weaknesses:** Difficult to generate precise quantitative predictions. Can be unfalsifiable if "meaning" is inferred post hoc.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves framing, public perception, stigma, or situations where "the story people tell" matters more than objective conditions.

### Prospect Theory / Behavioral Economics (Kahneman & Tversky)

- **Core idea:** People evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, are loss-averse, overweight small probabilities, and use heuristics that produce systematic biases.
- **Key mechanisms:** Loss aversion (losses hurt ~2x gains), status quo bias, anchoring, availability heuristic, endowment effect.
- **Predicts:** Resistance to change even when change is beneficial (loss aversion). Framing effects on risk-taking. Overreaction to vivid/salient events. Status quo persistence.
- **Strengths:** Well-validated experimentally. Precise predictions about decision-making under uncertainty.
- **Weaknesses:** Individual-level theory; requires aggregation logic for group/system predictions.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves decisions under uncertainty, reactions to losses or gains, risk assessment, or resistance to change.

## 2. Meso-Level Theories

### Institutional Theory (DiMaggio & Powell, Meyer & Rowan, Scott)

- **Core idea:** Organizations and actors are shaped by the institutional environment -- formal rules, informal norms, and taken-for-granted cultural beliefs. Organizations seek legitimacy, not just efficiency.
- **Key mechanisms:** Isomorphism (coercive, mimetic, normative), decoupling (formal compliance without substantive change), institutional logics, path dependence.
- **Predicts:** Organizations adopt practices to look legitimate, not because they work. New policies get formally adopted but substantively ignored (decoupling). Organizations in the same field converge on similar structures. Change is slow and path-dependent.
- **Strengths:** Explains persistence, conformity, and the gap between policy and practice. Very strong in organizational analysis.
- **Weaknesses:** Better at explaining stability than change. "Legitimacy" can be circular.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves organizational behavior, policy adoption, regulatory compliance, or understanding why formal changes don't produce expected effects.

### Field Theory (Bourdieu)

- **Core idea:** Social life occurs within structured "fields" where actors compete for various forms of capital (economic, cultural, social, symbolic). An actor's position in the field determines their strategies and chances.
- **Key mechanisms:** Capital accumulation and conversion, habitus (internalized dispositions), field position shaping strategy, symbolic violence (dominance accepted as natural).
- **Predicts:** Actors with more capital set the rules. Newcomers either conform to field rules or try to disrupt them (depending on their capital). Cultural and social capital often matter as much as economic capital. Dominant groups maintain advantage through subtle mechanisms, not just coercion.
- **Strengths:** Explains power dynamics, cultural hierarchies, and how advantage reproduces.
- **Weaknesses:** Difficult to operationalize "capital" and "field" precisely. Can become post hoc storytelling.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves competition, hierarchy, cultural dynamics, elite behavior, or understanding who has power and how they use it.

### Network Theory (Granovetter, Burt, Watts)

- **Core idea:** Social structure is best understood as networks of relationships. An actor's position in the network shapes their opportunities, information, and influence.
- **Key mechanisms:** Weak ties (bridges to new information), structural holes (brokerage advantage), network closure (trust and norm enforcement), diffusion through networks, preferential attachment.
- **Predicts:** Information and influence flow through network ties. Brokers (bridging structural holes) have disproportionate advantage. Diffusion follows network structure, not broadcast logic. Isolated actors are disadvantaged.
- **Strengths:** Precise, structural predictions. Can be quantified and tested.
- **Weaknesses:** Requires knowledge of actual network structure. Less useful when ties are weak or unknown.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves information flow, influence, diffusion (of innovations, behaviors, diseases), or when relationships between actors are central.

### Organizational Ecology (Hannan & Freeman)

- **Core idea:** Organizations are subject to selection pressures analogous to biological evolution. The environment selects for certain organizational forms; individual organizations have limited ability to adapt.
- **Key mechanisms:** Structural inertia, environmental selection, density dependence (legitimacy vs. competition), niche width.
- **Predicts:** Most organizational change attempts fail due to structural inertia. Environmental shifts kill organizations that cannot adapt, rather than transforming them. New organizational forms emerge to fill niches vacated by failed incumbents.
- **Strengths:** Explains organizational failure well. Provides a structural alternative to management voluntarism.
- **Weaknesses:** Somewhat fatalistic about organizational agency. Better at population-level than individual-organization predictions.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves organizational survival, industry disruption, competition between organizational forms, or why organizations fail to adapt.

## 3. Macro-Level Theories

### Structural Functionalism (Parsons, Merton)

- **Core idea:** Social systems are composed of interdependent parts that contribute to overall stability. When one part is disrupted, other parts adjust to restore equilibrium.
- **Key mechanisms:** Functional prerequisites (what a system needs to survive), manifest and latent functions, equilibrium-seeking, functional alternatives.
- **Predicts:** Disruptions trigger compensating adjustments elsewhere in the system. Social institutions persist because they serve functions (even if not the stated ones). Removing an institution without a functional alternative creates instability.
- **Strengths:** Good at explaining persistence and unintended consequences. Useful for thinking about system-level effects.
- **Weaknesses:** Can justify the status quo (everything exists because it's "functional"). Weak on explaining change and conflict.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves system-level disruptions, policy changes with unintended consequences, or understanding why things persist despite seeming dysfunctional.

### Conflict Theory (Marx, Weber, Collins)

- **Core idea:** Social life is fundamentally shaped by competition over scarce resources. Outcomes reflect power asymmetries between groups. Dominant groups structure institutions to preserve their advantage.
- **Key mechanisms:** Power asymmetry, resource competition, ideological legitimation (dominant groups make their interests appear universal), class/status/party as distinct dimensions of stratification.
- **Predicts:** Policies nominally aimed at equity will be captured or co-opted by powerful groups. Change requires mobilization by disadvantaged groups. "Neutral" institutions reproduce existing inequalities. Resistance from dominant groups intensifies when their position is threatened.
- **Strengths:** Explains inequality, power dynamics, and why seemingly progressive changes often fail to redistribute.
- **Weaknesses:** Can be reductive (everything is about power). May overpredict conflict in stable situations.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves power dynamics, inequality, policy reform, resource distribution, or when outcomes systematically favor one group.

### World-Systems Theory (Wallerstein)

- **Core idea:** The global economy is a single system with core, semi-peripheral, and peripheral zones. Core nations extract surplus from the periphery through unequal exchange.
- **Key mechanisms:** Unequal exchange, core-periphery hierarchy, commodity chains, hegemonic cycles.
- **Predicts:** Economic development in the periphery is constrained by core extraction. Mobility between zones is rare. Global shifts reflect hegemonic transitions, not convergence.
- **Strengths:** Good for analyzing global inequality, trade dynamics, geopolitical shifts.
- **Weaknesses:** Very macro -- hard to apply to local or organizational scenarios. Deterministic.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves international trade, global inequality, geopolitics, or core-periphery dynamics.

### Cultural Theory (Swidler, Alexander)

- **Core idea:** Culture is not just values and beliefs but a "toolkit" of symbols, stories, rituals, and practices that actors use to construct strategies of action. Cultural change happens when new toolkits become available or old ones lose relevance.
- **Key mechanisms:** Cultural toolkits, settled vs. unsettled periods (culture matters more when things are in flux), narrative framing, ritual, symbolic boundaries.
- **Predicts:** Behavior changes when the cultural toolkit changes, not just when incentives change. In unsettled periods (crises, transitions), culture is highly contested and ideological. New narratives can reshape behavior even without material changes.
- **Strengths:** Explains cultural change and the role of meaning in shaping behavior beyond interests.
- **Weaknesses:** Hard to predict which cultural toolkit wins in contested periods.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves cultural change, narrative conflict, meaning-making, or periods of social upheaval.

## 4. Cross-Level Theories

### Diffusion of Innovations (Rogers)

- **Core idea:** Innovations spread through populations in a predictable S-curve pattern. Adoption depends on innovation characteristics (relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, observability) and adopter categories (innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards).
- **Key mechanisms:** S-curve diffusion, opinion leadership, critical mass, reinvention during adoption.
- **Predicts:** Early adoption by a small group; acceleration after critical mass; eventual saturation or abandonment. Complex or incompatible innovations diffuse slowly. Social influence drives adoption more than information.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves spread of a new practice, technology, policy, or behavior.

### Actor-Network Theory (Latour, Callon)

- **Core idea:** The social world is made up of networks of human and non-human actors (machines, texts, laws, standards). Outcomes depend on how these heterogeneous actors are enrolled and aligned.
- **Key mechanisms:** Translation (redefining interests to align actors), enrollment, obligatory passage points, black-boxing (making complex networks look like single actors).
- **Predicts:** Outcomes depend on successfully enrolling all relevant actors (including technological ones). Failed projects fail because they could not align the network. Technology is never purely technical -- it carries social arrangements within it.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves technology implementation, infrastructure, standardization, or situations where non-human actors (algorithms, devices, regulations) shape outcomes.

### Practice Theory (Bourdieu, Giddens, Schatzki)

- **Core idea:** Social life is constituted by practices -- routinized patterns of bodily and mental activity. Practices are more fundamental than either individuals or structures.
- **Key mechanisms:** Routinization, practical consciousness (knowing how without knowing that), structuration (practices reproduce and transform structures), material arrangements enabling/constraining practice.
- **Predicts:** Change is hard because practices are deeply embodied and routinized. Top-down mandates fail when they conflict with existing practice. New practices emerge when material arrangements change.
- **Use when:** The scenario involves behavior change, habit, routine, or understanding why people keep doing things despite knowing better.

## 5. Selection Guidance

### By scenario type

| Scenario type | Recommended theories |
|---|---|
| Organizational change | Institutional theory, rational choice, organizational ecology, practice theory |
| Policy intervention | Rational choice, institutional theory, conflict theory, prospect theory |
| Intergroup conflict | Social identity theory, conflict theory, network theory |
| Technology adoption | Diffusion of innovations, actor-network theory, institutional theory |
| Market competition | Rational choice, field theory, organizational ecology |
| Cultural shift | Cultural theory, symbolic interactionism, diffusion of innovations |
| Community dynamics | Network theory, social identity theory, structural functionalism |
| Geopolitical | World-systems theory, conflict theory, rational choice |
| Inequality / stratification | Conflict theory, field theory, institutional theory |

### Diversity rules

- Always include at least one theory that predicts **stability/persistence** and one that predicts **change/disruption**
- Always include at least one theory that focuses on **individual agency** and one on **structural constraint**
- If all selected theories agree, add one that disagrees -- consensus is uninteresting
